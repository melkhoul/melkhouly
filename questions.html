How I Think

The decisions behind the work.

The work I value most rarely began with an obvious solution. It began with a decision: what problem are we actually solving, what evidence should change our view, where should we invest, and what should we stop doing? These selected stories show how I approach those choices.

AI-enabled workflow transformation

What if a 7.5-month process was an operating-model problem, not simply a resourcing problem?

Operating-model redesign

Learning-development requests were taking an average of 7.5 months. The visible constraint was capacity, but adding resources would have treated the symptom. I reframed the problem around the operating model: how could the organization fundamentally change the way work moved from an identified performance need to a usable solution?

I influenced senior leadership to establish a reduction goal and secured agreement to create the function’s first scrum-based delivery team. Rather than beginning with a preferred technology, we evaluated multiple internal and external options, selected an AI-enabled approach, tested it with leaders and built adoption into the operating mechanism.

The redesigned workflow reduced foundational-content design time by 99% and overall soft-skills turnaround by 60%. The broader lesson was structural: technology creates disproportionate value when leaders are willing to redesign the system around it—not simply insert it into the existing process.

Digitization · evidence before preference

The delivery model was heavily instructor-led, making facilitation time a significant source of non-productive capacity. Digitizing system training appeared to be the obvious answer, but the proposal was challenged by a senior leader. Rather than argue from preference, I turned the disagreement into an experiment.

I designed an A/B test comparing delivery approaches and used learner retention alongside operating efficiency as decision evidence. In the tested context, the digital approach produced 37% higher retention while reducing facilitation time by 45%.

The significance was not simply moving training online. It established a pattern I continue to use: when senior stakeholders hold different views and the available evidence is weak, make the assumption testable and let evidence determine the direction.

Sherlock · governance & cost

The organization had set a significant training-cost reduction objective, initially framed around reducing seat time. I began with the baseline rather than the proposed solution, analyzing approximately 13,000 data points. The analysis exposed a more fundamental issue: the learning function had visibility of only about a quarter of the activity being classified as training, with meetings also contributing to the cost base.

I concluded that sustainable savings required governance, not another isolated efficiency intervention. When a key stakeholder raised bandwidth and capability constraints, I negotiated dedicated ownership, designed the workflow and enabled the new owner to operate it. The mechanism introduced clearer enrollment and oversight rather than relying on informal control.

The work delivered a reported 400K reduction. More importantly, it reinforced a leadership principle: when the system lacks visibility and ownership, optimizing individual activities will not solve the underlying economics.

Capability strategy · decision architecture

As my scope expanded, individual learning solutions stopped being the right unit of analysis. The harder problem was how a function makes coherent choices across populations, programs, metrics, people, technology, governance and investment.

I developed a strategy mechanism that connects organizational context, business goals and needs analysis to the choices required to build a multi-year capability strategy—including programs, metrics, staffing, technology, skills, governance and estimated investment.

The intention was not to create another planning template. It was to make strategic choices explicit enough for leaders to align stakeholders, expose trade-offs and connect capability investment to organizational priorities.

AI adoption · from access to behavior

Why doesn’t giving people AI tools automatically change how they work?

Click to read the story ↻
AI adoption · provisional evidence

Tool availability is only one condition for adoption. I started by measuring actual usage and barriers rather than assuming that more training was the answer, then combined targeted capability building, peer examples and collaborative work on real pain points.

Among 22 post-initiative respondents, reported daily AI use increased from 48% to 68%, while reported non-use fell from four people to zero. I treat that as evidence of an early adoption intervention—not proof of organization-wide transformation.

The work sharpened a distinction that now anchors my approach. Adoption asks, “How can AI help us do what we already do?” Transformation asks, “What should we do differently because AI exists?” My current thinking therefore looks beyond capability building to leadership, governance, infrastructure, behavior, workflow change and business outcomes.
